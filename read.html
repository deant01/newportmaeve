PART 1: THE OLD DAYS

Back in the good old days.

Dion had heard the phrase more times than he could count. Even the long-lived elves, who watched centuries drift by like passing seasons, were not immune to nostalgia’s lie. Dion never yielded. He had outlived kings and tribes, watched cities rise and vanish, and listened to the same old lament: "Things were better once."

He had lived long enough to know the truth. The "good old days" were never good. And yet, centuries later, even the worst of them wore a golden sheen in hindsight. That, too, was part of survival. Forgetting just enough.

He’d survived where others didn’t. Adapted when the world shifted. Lost everything, rebuilt, then lost it all again. What kept him going wasn’t hope. It was the stubborn instinct not to stop.

Dion remembered the first memory that truly mattered. It broke him open and reassembled him into something unrecognizable. This was the birth of who he became, a soul forged entirely by pain and blood.

It was in the far east, lifetimes ago. He’d been a foot soldier then. Just another soul thrown into a war no one would win. What had started as a trade dispute between two ambitious tribes had become a decade of bloodletting. It was a childish feud escalated by the foolish pride of rulers too arrogant to face one another without violence.

Dion fought first out of duty, then simply to stay alive. The war had devoured everything. Desolation swept through the tribal villages, erasing entire generations and leaving no futures behind. There was nowhere left to go, nothing left to believe in. The conflict had bled the land dry, turning fields into graveyards and rivers into veins of rot. Each battle blurred into the next, until even victory lost its meaning.

Resources went thin, the ranks thinner. Gathering whatever last reserves they had, the tribes met in one final clash. The one that would devour what little remained. Whoever stood last would claim it all. The rest would be buried beneath.

The field was churned to mud by boots and blood. Dion stumbled forward with an aching body and a sword slick with grime. He’d killed whom he could, fled from those stronger, and kept moving, one breath at a time. Lean and elegant rather than imposing, he stood a little taller than most, his frame shaped more by endurance than raw power. Dark brown hair, streaked with silver at the temples, clung damply to his brow above a face defined by a prominent nose, thin lips drawn into something far from a smile, and a beard shaved down with rough neglect. His cold, gray-blue eyes swept across the chaos before him. The fighting had scattered his unit. Following the sounds of shouting and steel to the hilltop, he arrived mere seconds after the rival chieftain struck down Dion’s own leader.

He was so close he could still see the twitch in the slain man's fingers, as the breath left his pierced lungs.

The chieftain stood with arms raised in triumph, his warriors roaring around him. Defeat broke Dion’s bloodied comrades. They sank to their knees, their weapons falling like whispers into the muck.

He stopped. This would not resolve anything. This would only make it worse. The solution crystallized in his tired mind.

No one saw him slip from the haze.

No one thought the battle had another breath left.

But it did.

Dion stepped behind the chieftain, reached out, and drew his blade across the man’s throat.

The cheers died mid-breath, a single heartbeat stretched thin. The warriors who had roared now stood mute, the sound stolen from their throats. Even the wounded in the mud seemed to pause their cries. A cold silence fell over the battlefield.

The chieftain’s body pitched forward, landing face-first in the red muck. Steam rose from the blood as if the earth itself recoiled.

Dion stood above the stiffening corpse, his chest heaving, throat raw, every nerve trembling. The field was suspended in stillness, as if the world itself held its breath. He looked into the wide, stunned eyes of the survivors, enemy and ally alike. In their gaze, he saw confusion, fear, and a desperate need for meaning.

And so he gave it to them.

He let it all spill out, a lifetime of buried rage and grief bleeding into the hollow desperation of a survivor who had seen too much and lived anyway.

“They were both fools!” he roared. “And we were worse—fools who offered our blood to crown their vanity! Look around you! Our homes are cinders! Our sons are bones beneath this mud! Our daughters mourn ghosts! We gave everything to feed their pride, and what did we earn?”

He gestured to the two corpses beneath him. “Let them rot. Let the flies crown them. Let their legacy be silence. Now go home. In peace.”

Then he dropped his sword.

Then his helmet.

He turned his back on the war.

And walked away.

Behind him came the sound of steel hitting soil. Soldiers, on both sides, turned their backs to the dead.

Dion didn’t look back. Behind him lay the ash of every illusion he’d once clung to, from loyalty to friendship. Before him stretched nothing but the unknown. His wife had long since vanished into memory, taken by a fever that no healer’s hand could touch. Her absence had hollowed him out, leaving only momentum in its place. He walked to escape his grief, with no destination in mind, only the need to be elsewhere. To be away.

And in some perverted way, fate responded. Amused, he watched how a leaf carried by the wind slowed its flight unnaturally. In a sudden blink, it stopped. Time itself also stopped, and he could no longer move. His thoughts were still flowing, but everything else froze. It felt as if he had stepped out of the normal world, yet he was still able to observe it. Then came a sudden release. He felt his body again, but he was no longer alone.

A figure stood before him, tall, bare-chested, and sculpted like a statue. Its skin was slick and black, like oil under starlight. Golden symbols shimmered across its surface, glowing faintly. Hair like woven fire fell behind its elongated ears. Lacking pupils or irises, its eyes were like molten amber, smooth fields of radiant energy staring into Dion’s thoughts.

“Miroslav Dion,” it said. It's voice was smooth as marble, but the way it spoke was almost mechanical, like reading a sentence. “You are suitable.”

Dion blinked. “Suitable for what?”

“For the Blood Blessing.”

The words meant nothing, but something in them chilled him.

“Within you, it will be sharpened like a blade and preserved against the erosion of time. The One True has foreseen your path, Dion. You will bear this gift across the centuries as its steward and guard it from rot, even when the world around you falls to ruin.”

Dion stepped back. “I don’t want any blessing. I don’t even know what ‘One True’ is. Leave me be!”

The figure tilted its head. “To be what?”

The question didn't land right, but Dion barely gave it a thought as panic started rising.

He stammered, “To be a free man.”

“I offer exactly that,” the being said, stepping closer. “Freedom. And it will not end in the grave. It will stretch far beyond your given time."

Dion took one more step back. “There’s a catch. There’s always a catch.”

“That is why we chose you,” it said, its tone colder now. “You are not like the others. You question. You calculate. That is precisely why you will endure. But understand this: the gift is not free. A moment will come when you will be called to act. And you will obey.”

Dion’s breath caught. His gut turned to stone. “No,” he whispered, then louder, more desperate, “No. This isn’t right. I refuse! I won’t be your pawn!”

For the first time, the being smiled, slightly amused. “You presume the One True is asking.”

It raised a small metallic instrument. Dion’s eyes widened, his instincts screamed for action, but he was too slow. There was a sharp hiss, followed by a sting in his thigh.

His knees buckled. The piercing pain and the sheer absurdity of the turn stunned him completely. One moment he had defied a god, and the next, he was a mere vessel.

Then the true pain came.

It detonated through his flesh like fire laced with razors. A searing wave tore through sinew and bone, consuming breath and thought alike. Dion screamed as the world shattered into shards of light and torment.

A rising tempest of anguish and fear drowned his mind. Through the cacophony, he barely registered the voice: "Let the One True have mercy on your soul." It was distant, like an echo carried through water.

Then came the fall.

Then darkness.

When he woke, the sky felt distant. The air smelled wrong. The world was slower, quieter and the sun burned his skin.

And he was no longer the same.

He had to learn how to walk again. How to hunt and pretend.

He had become a predator, and now he had to learn to live among the cattle, to wear a man’s skin while his nature whispered hunger beneath.

PART 2: THE VAMPIRE'S DAWN

There was no manual or scripture, nor whispered wisdom from the stars.

Dion awoke to a world that had not changed, but to which he no longer belonged. People moved somewhat slower, and their heartbeats rang in his ears like drums.

And there was The Thirst. For their blood.

At first, he gave in. He threaded through villages and outposts, taking life and leaving corpses. Sometimes he didn't remember the moments in between. Sometimes he did, and that was worse.

People called him names from the old stories. Vampyry — bad spirits that fed on the innocent. In desperate prayers to the old deities, they begged for salvation. From Dion, and from those born in his shadow.

They were lost souls turned by his blood in moments of hunger and confusion. Feral things with no remorse that tore through settlements without thought. Word spread and then pitchforks followed.

The chaos that ignited snapped Dion back to his senses. He stood in the mud, covered in blood, his mind strangely clear. This path led to annihilation and it had to stop. For that, he had to hunt his own children. One by one.

Horrified, he then understood the true nature of his existence. The Blood Blessing was hard to contain. The affected became monsters with almost no weaknesses, except sunlight. And the sun hurt him too. So he fought them in the dark, each fight on the brink of his own end. He found that their kind’s rapid healing and unnatural endurance were hard to break. Maybe the fact he was the first, and likely the strongest, was the one thing that helped. He wore them down, beheading some, catching others in blazing traps that turned them to ash.

And when the fires died, he was alone again. But he was no longer blind.

He starved himself to learn discipline. Buried himself in libraries, abandoned shrines, forgotten towers. Centuries passed in exile. And slowly, he began to understand: the thirst would never leave, but it could be caged.

When he next turned another, it was no accident. He watched them for months, sometimes years, assessing them carefully. He searched for individuals who could bear the burden without going mad. Individuals who would be worth granting prolonged life, the way that damned Entity chose him. When they woke in darkness, he cared for them, then taught them discipline and control. And most importantly—he gave them purpose.

He had already learned what chaos brings. Now, he searched for order and control. From this blueprint, a community grew. Call it kin or a family, but it was the thing he needed most: a stable structure.

Among the first to thrive was Mariana Voss, a medicine woman whose devotion to healing outweighed even her own humanity. To her, blood was sacred, and also data. An uncharted map of the body that waited to be read. Becoming one of Dion’s kind was a calculated wager that eternity might buy her the time science needed. She saw the nature of the hunger and sought a solution. A cure, she found, was impossible. The Blood Blessing had rooted itself deep into the carrier, reshaping their marrow and flesh in profound ways. Alongside the endless thirst and severe sunlight intolerance, it granted a vastly extended life and enhanced abilities, raising their strength and speed far beyond the peak of any common race. And the virus adapted. It could survive and convert almost any living being. That is why they had to keep it restrained… and fed. From her design came the first Blood Clinics.

On paper, they were a public good for the sick and the anemic. In truth, they provided a voluntary flow of lifeblood to sustain Dion’s kin. No deaths or fear. And no pitchforks ever again.

The effect was startling. Medicine surged forward, hematological research flourished. Lives were saved while the Blood Blessed endured. A compromise, of sorts.

Dion moved through time like a sculptor. Refining and adjusting, he watched the Confederacy grow from fractured provinces to organized country. As bureaucracy bloomed, the legal codes deepened. That was when he found his second passion.

Law.

To Dion, the law was not a chain. It was a map, drawn by fools, but legible to him. A mind honed by centuries in search of control could see the bigger picture, where short-lived humans and shorter-mandated politicians struggled. Even the elves, with more years to their existence, failed to match his scope. Laughing at its inner contradictions, he easily skirted every blind assumption to carve his own hidden path.

Where the law ended, his network began.

The Underground. Forgotten old tunnels and newly invented sewers were just a minor part of the machine. Its real structure was its people and their ties. Simple back-alley robbers fed information to sophisticated fixers and brokers. Ruthless silencers and cleaners finished the jobs the law could not touch. Expensive? Yes, but also remarkably flawless and effective.

Peace was maintained precisely because Dion held both scales.

Decades passed, then centuries. Dion witnessed the sudden leap in progress that followed the creation of the Confederacy. Its people, though divided by race and prejudice, somehow started to work together. With no need to constantly look over their shoulders for war, scholars were free to explore other fields of science.

In one absurd coincidence, they discovered the power of steam. Within a decade or so, they stabilized the technology and began building factories driven by steam-powered machines. They didn’t stop there. Enormous drilling rigs struck endless pockets of natural gas, and only a few more years later, the fuel hissed through pipes coiling toward the rich delta of the Zarina River. Right where the river kissed the coast of the Moon Sea, the Confederacy built its crown jewel.

Newport Maeve.

It was a city of brass and invention, built with the ambition to be a testbed for progress and a beacon to all those who still believed in the future.

Dion watched it rise. But he did more than just watch. He funded parts of its growth, seeding ties and influence in the process. A key law pushed here, a specific person placed there—these were the levers of control he knew he must have.

Yet, there was something else. This was a city unlike any other on the continent, miraculous and flawed at the same time. In his own way, Dion admired it.

It was then that the call came.

A voice, not heard in centuries.

A promise made long ago, one he had never wished to make.

The moment had arrived.

The debt was due.

PART 3: UNWANTED DEBT

At the time, Dion’s primary operations were centered in the capital of the Confederacy. The city of Cairndor sprawled at the foot of the Cairn Peaks, surrounded by mountains and plateaus that left only two easily controlled passes through its crests. Its towers rose like spears of stone against the crags. Shadowed streets wound through a tapestry of classical halls with marble colonnades and extended across vaulted bridges that connected the sides of cold brooks, which swelled into rushing rivers down the outside valleys. This ancient architecture was built deep into the mountain base and was meant to outlast centuries. Among the newer golden statues of the One True, there still lurked older monuments of ancient dragons and giant behemoths. These noble beasts, long vanished from the world, were carved in gray granite, softened by wind and rain but never forgotten.

At the city’s heart lay the wide square, flanked on one side by the Palace of the Seven. The vast edifice of domes and pillars was raised to honor the nations that had once set aside their quarrels to forge the Confederacy. Facing it across the open expanse stood the Sun Cathedral, all soaring glass and gold, built in the aftermath of the demon wars as a monument to the faith born in blood and fire.

It was in that square, long ago, that the new faith had first taken root. Dion remembered the day well. A black-winged demon tore the crimson sky open, but just when the mortal defenders were almost vanquished, angelic figures descended to drive it back in a blaze of light. That spectacle had ignited the Church of the One True, now risen to power across the land.

But memory was a crueler scripture. Dion had been there, had seen more than the faithful ever would. And though the city bent its knee, he never had. He remembered too much to believe.

So when the invitation came, it brought more suspicion than awe.

It was just after twilight. The hooves of his carriage halted in front of his company’s headquarters. The building was an old administrative tenement, left to crumble unattended until he bought it from City Hall. After patching it up, he gave the Consortium signature colors of pure black and vivid gold. The sidewalk tiles in front had been replaced with fresh masonry, and on them, standing in the glow of a street oil lamp, was a figure in clerical robes. He was an elf, serene in expression but tense in posture, and the bald patch at his left temple marked him as Bishop Arsiwald Tearstream.

Dion stepped out with two aides flanking him. He didn't like the high-ranking priest, and the feelings were mutual. In the front, the harnessed horses wheezed, shivering as if they could sense his sudden discomfort.

The bishop was not alone. Two fully armored paladins stood at his side. Their helmets were on, but their weapons and shields were secured to their backs. They didn't talk, just watched his group coldly. He knew their elven eyes see everything, even in the darkening twilight.

Dion stood still, watching them back. He'd been in enough ambushes to know the scent of a veiled threat. The paladins weren’t only muscle. They were a warning the Church preferred not to speak in words. This was a conversation he couldn't avoid without triggering alarms in places that needed to remain silent.

“Mister Dion,” the bishop greeted with a placid smile. “May the One True Light be with you.”

“Father Arsiwald,” Dion replied flatly. “Likewise.”

First name only. Insulting. The bishop’s smile faltered, but he pressed on. “We are sent to invite you to the Sun Cathedral. For a High Prayer.”

Dion’s eyebrow arched. “Father, with all due respect, you know I walk the free path.”

The ‘free path’. A term for those who did not adhere to faith, but remained within the law by formally rejecting all other gods, cults, or esoteric beliefs. It was a tolerated stance in the Confederacy, so long as the individual placed no devotion in rival doctrines. Believing in nothing was permitted. Believing in something else was heresy, and the paladins were swift in dealing with it.

With a slight turn of his head, Arsiwald looked at the armored elves beside him. Without a word, they moved forward in unison. Their heavy boots struck the sidewalk tiles in a single deliberate step, and the metallic clamor of plate armor echoed like a warning bell through the quiet street.

The bishop's eyes fixed on Dion's. “Look, Mr. Dion. The High Prayer is not a matter of belief. It is a matter of summons. A person-specific rite, issued only by the highest authority of the Church. It requires the invited to attend, regardless of faith or creed. Think of it less as a spiritual request and more as... a diplomatic obligation.”

Dion raised a hand to his guards, halting them and waving away the smallest possibility of confrontation. A crooked smile touched his lips. “You make me feel special, Father,” he said mockingly, then added, “When is this divine performance scheduled to begin?”

“As soon as we reach the Sun Cathedral,” the bishop replied, recovering his formal tone.

Dion turned to re-enter his carriage. “Lead the way.”

“Your carriage will go ahead,” Arsiwald corrected coldly. “Ours will follow. You know the way.”

Dion sighed. “Of course I do.”

The ornate double doors of Divine Chamber Twelve loomed ahead, gold-gilded and silent. The corridor that led there was lined with stained glass and polished marble, but no warmth lived within. The glass depicted saints and saviors. Some of those Dion remembered instead as murderers, exploiters, and frauds dressed in sanctity. History, he thought, had a generous hand.

“This is your chamber, Mr. Dion,” Arsiwald said. His voice held the usual ceremony, but his pointy ears twitched. It was barely perceptible, but there. Nervous.

“I go in alone?” Dion asked, amused.

“The one leading the High Prayer awaits within.” The bishop lowered his voice, suddenly sharp. “Afraid, Miroslav?”

Dion straightened, caught off guard by the bishop’s uncharacteristically blunt jab. It was a sharp break from the polished protocol he’d expected and Dion, adaptive as usual, answered in kind. His voice dripped with mockery, his grin unfazed. “Oh, my pants are trembling, Arsiwald, my boy.”

The elf flushed red, turned with a flourish of his robes, and marched off.

Dion shrugged, placed a hand on the door’s handle, and pushed.

What he saw nearly made him stumble.

The room was round, the walls covered in celestial motifs and religious iconography. And there, sitting cross-legged atop the divine altar like a casual trespasser—was him.

The entity.

Black skin, golden glyphs. Hair like woven flame. The same calm face. The same presence.

A grin spread across the entity’s face as Dion froze in the doorway.

“What the hell are you doing here?” Dion hissed, his fangs slipping out involuntarily. “Do you have any idea how many centuries I’ve wanted to find you? To kill you?”

The entity tilted his head, then returned to his impassive mask.

“I told you the One True chose you because you’re smart,” he said. “Yes—hell is why you’re here.”

“You speak in riddles again,” Dion growled.

“Hell,” the entity repeated, “is reaching out once more.”

Dion’s expression turned to stone.

He remembered. The demon with his hellspawn army. The fire. The sky torn open. The angels that followed. It had started everything.

“And what does that have to do with me?”

“You made a promise,” the entity said.

“No... A promise was made on my behalf,” Dion snapped.

The entity might have rolled its eyes if it had pupils. “Semantics.”

“Newport Maeve,” the entity continued, “is a thin point. Between realities. It’s there the breach will happen.”

Dion scowled. “What makes that city any different from the rest? Why Newport Maeve, of all places?”

The entity gave him a hard, long look, as if he was an infant barely seeing past his nose. “Because it is the pinnacle. The crowning monument to the progress of the mortals. The most technologically advanced city of your age. Where ambition walks hand in hand with hubris. And progress,” he said slowly, “breeds corruption. Corruption blossoms into indulgence. And indulgence—it sharpens hunger into obsession, polishes desire until it gleams. The hellspawn, Dion... they do not feed on blood or flesh. They feed on that refinement, on the cravings shaped by civilization itself.”

“You’re joking,” Dion muttered.

“They’ve already made their move.”

Silence.

“This is your call, Dion. You must go. Settle there.”

“To do what, exactly?” Dion asked, irritated.

The entity’s gaze remained steady. “To do what you were shaped to do,” it said. “To adapt. To bend your surroundings to your will. To infiltrate the veins of the city until its pulse beats in rhythm with yours. Order, influence, and control will follow, as they always have.”

The moment stretched. Dion caught himself waiting for more. There should be more. Something unspoken passed between them, perhaps summoned by the look in Dion’s eyes or the weight of the silence itself.

The entity’s voice carried a rare weight, stripped of mystery, as if pulling back a veil. “There will be no angelic host this time. No divine army to descend in fire and song. The One True is... preoccupied. With other matters, beyond your understanding. The peoples of this world... They are distracted. Tangled in their machines, their politics, their petty wars. They see the signs, yet dismiss them.”

He paused. His eyes, empty of iris and yet full of knowing, bore into Dion's. “Think of yourself as the One True’s contingency. The backup plan. When no gods can intervene, it falls to mortals to act. And you, Dion, must help. You will aid the One True by defending the people of this world. They still show promise. As you once did.”

A brief silence lingered like smoke.

Dion chuckled dryly. “Was the spectacle with the bishop and the High Prayer necessary?” he asked.

The entity offered one of his rare, wry smiles. “Within the Cathedral, I can enter your world without drawing attention,” he said. “And besides, don’t you find it amusing? How seriously they treat all of this?” His grin widened, a flicker of ancient mischief in his eyes. “Living long enough breeds a very particular boredom, as you well know.”

Dion turned serious again. “One question. You haven’t changed in all this time. Did you use the virus on yourself?”

The entity smirked. “No... I just know how to cut through time.”

He stood. The glyphs on his skin pulsed faintly.

“When you arrive in Newport Maeve, you will be greeted by its protector. Be kind to her," the entity added, with an odd flicker of emotion. Something almost close to regret. "She bears more weight than you’ll see.”

“Who is she?”

But the entity was already fading.

“Farewell, Dion. See you in a millennium.”

The last outline of his silhouette melted and he was gone.

Once again, Dion was left with more questions than answers.

Part 4: Arrival in the city

Dion, of course, already had one foot in the new metropolis. He was still in the capital, but he had sent his vanguard to Newport Maeve to prepare the ground for his operations. The Ironclad Consortium, his law firm, had established a growing branch there, meticulously managing civil and corporate disputes and rooting itself deep into the forming political and economic cityscape. The Blood Clinic was already operating, as were his “informational scouts,” as he liked to call them. These were specialists, watchers in plain sight, charting the city’s undercurrents. They observed it all: from petty theft and brawls on the streets, to high end contract killings, laundering schemes, and racketeering webs run by whoever sat at the top of the food chain.

When patterns emerged, so did they. Backed by Dion's almost limitless resources, bolstered by the sharpened instincts and strength his blood gave them, they acted. If a street gang grew too bold, they toppled it. If a fraud scheme grew too greedy, they subverted it. And they never left the vacuum empty. They filled it themselves. The system was still in motion, and Dion intended to move in only once it was near completion. But the events got ahead of him.

He had climbed high enough on the ladder of influence to speak directly to the Leading Consul of the Confederacy and strike a bargain. Dion would deliver them a controlled underworld. In return, his society would remain untouched. But no government sat across a table from something like him without wanting an edge of its own, and the Consul was no exception. Dion signed anyway. He'd sworn off arming other men's wars once, a lifetime ago, on a field he still remembered the smell of. Apparently that oath had an expiration date too. The contract they signed was harder than tool steel, and he would make the best of it, like he always did.

Now, legs crossed and a whiskey glass in hand, Dion gazed through the small shaded window of his ornate carriage. The filter dulled the twilight sun’s irritating glare without spoiling the view. Steam vehicles were still in their infancy, used mainly for short hauls within city limits. For a journey such as this, two prime-bred, two-tailed horses pulled him steadily toward Newport Maeve.

Several decades had passed since the Confederacy had embarked on this venture. Construction surged and new buildings rose daily. Fresh neighborhoods stretched outward like fingers grasping the future. Just when the sun dipped behind the Moon Sea, the carriage slowed at the looming checkpoint gate, set into the half-completed outer wall of concrete and steel.

A barked order rang out from the officer in charge. The carriage halted.

“Elise,” Dion said, glancing at his valet, sitting across from him.

Ebony-skinned, she had a graceful, athletic figure. The dark green tailored suit she wore contrasted with the red-dyed hair she had gathered in a high tail. Her curls shifted, and her hazel eyes turned to meet his, answering the call.

“Yes, Mr. Dion,” she replied with a small nod. The sun was almost gone, but she pulled her protective hood just in case, then stepped down from the carriage with documents in hand. Credentials, too valuable to entrust to the driver.

A few minutes passed, during which low words were exchanged at the gate. Then Elise reentered the carriage, pulling down her hood, before she slid gracefully into the seat opposite him.

“Welcome to Newport Maeve, sir,” she said with a bright smile, showing perfectly aligned white teeth, two of which were just slightly longer than they should be.

“Let’s see what all the fuss is about, Elise,” Dion murmured, a faint smirk tugging at his mouth.

They passed through the threshold, and the city opened before them, washing them in a riot of sound and movement. Steam cranes swung their last loads for the passing day overhead. Below them, trucks groaned under loads of stone and steel, and all around, workers swarmed the streets like ants in a hive. Here and there, on rectangular elevated platforms, well-dressed architects in protective helmets were constantly checking the blueprints unrolled in their hands, making sure each construction piece fell into place for the city's grand design.

Dion had seen many cities rise, but he had never seen one built by people who expected to win. Other cities were built to survive, raising thick walls first, to protect themselves from the chaos outside. Newport Maeve left the walls last. It built factories instead, and dared the future to show up and take a look. The people here were thinking differently. They were harnessing the progress in every niche of their existence. Dion could do the same. He had adapted for millennia, learning new ways each time the world shifted beneath him. His cold heart skipped one of its slow beats as he finally understood what this place could become. He could belong here, or the city could belong to him. Semantics. He smiled. That damned Entity.

In the north, the Market District stood largely complete, as did much of the stretch toward the Moon Sea. There, the Ironclad Tower, his new headquarters, awaited him.

The first week was a blur of formalities. Lieutenant Attorney Vasko Delie, Dion's current head of Ironclad Operations in the city, introduced him to Mayor Filion Dyne, and to Archbishop Tobin Goodstar. The head of the Church Body was an ancient elf still chasing his thousandth year. Dion had only to listen to the Archbishop’s heartbeat to know he would not see it.

Goodstar was a gentle soul, ill-suited to a city like Newport Maeve. Age made him slow to change. His congregation was adrift, and his authority was fading. On top of that, his relations with Marshal Zarin Leah, the middle-aged elf and paladin commander, were notoriously strained. Perhaps that explained the heretic cults that slipped constantly through the city’s seams.

Dion reminded himself it was not his concern. It had never been. He wasn't on good terms with the Church, though in his own way he also served the One True. For reasons neither side could name, they provoked one another on sight and the friction was as inevitable as it was enduring. Time would tell if Newport Maeve would be any different.

Part 5: Encounter on the rooftop

One Sunday night, Dion, sick of another boring corporate case, decided he wanted out. Clean air was too much to ask in a steam-choked metropolis. The open space of the terrace of his apartment on top of the Ironclad Tower would have to do. Leaning on its brass railing he inhaled, then frowned and coughed once. His administrative duties kept pulling him away from what actually mattered. If he meant to control Newport Maeve, he would need more than political maps and ledgers. He needed to feel the heartbeat of its alleys and the slanted rooftops under his boots.

Dion looked at the full moon partly covered by residual steam clouds and smiled. His Blessing, or maybe curse, had taken the sun away from him. This was his eternal companion now. He stepped onto the railing and jumped over.

It was time for a walk.

He slipped across the skyline as though woven into it, his preternatural speed carrying him across the many levels of the city, even through the larger arteries of the sewers. Learning the language of waterpipes and gargoyles, he marked important places and choke points until he could navigate the grid blindly.

Then he met her.

The Protector.

Even his sharpened senses hadn’t warned him. One moment, the moonlit rooftop above the gleaming new docks was empty, the next, she was simply there. Tall and lean, her long raven hair fell around a pale face, both beautiful and cold. Dark wings folded neatly behind her. She wore a fitted combat suit studded with metal chains and reinforced straps serving as protection and, probably, some marker of rank. Her hands rested lightly on the hilt of a two-handed sword. Its tip was planted on the rooftop tiles, the blade veined with a faint golden glow that mirrored the rippling lights from the harbor far below.

Dion was surprised, but he let none of it show. He stood as he always did, calm and cold, waiting.

“So you’re the Curse Carrier,” she said flatly. “I’m not impressed.”

Her tone was edged with the arrogance of youth. Dion guessed she was either new to her post or young by whatever measure such beings used.

“Well, Protector,” he replied, “curse is the right term. But I am here to do my job. As you are, I presume.”

She shifted her stance, lifting the greatsword onto her shoulder with a metallic ring. “I am perfectly capable of doing what I do without you darting around…” she leaned forward slightly, the golden light catching in her eyes, “…leech.”

Dion’s temper flared, but he kept it buried. “And what exactly is it you do?” he asked. “The city is calm. No devils, no demons. Paladins patrol constantly.”

“It seems calm because I do my job,” she countered with a smirk. “But I don’t want you turning citizens into leeches like yourself.” Her expression cooled. “That would complicate my work, and we don’t want that.”

He was on the verge of snapping when a memory slid into place: black skin, glowing eyes, and a voice. Be kind to her.

Very well.

“Listen, Protector,” he said, pressing the title with deliberate weight. “It will not happen. I have a strict protocol for who can be what in my society.” He gestured faintly to the city around them. “If you find otherwise, you can seek me out again—and we can have any kind of conversation you wish.”

The meaning was clear. She found it amusing. Her smile widened, bright and perfect, and for the briefest flicker of a second he allowed himself to acknowledge her beauty.

“Your name is Dion, right? Miroslav Dion?”

He inclined his head in a simple nod.

“Now I’m impressed,” she said and for just a secon, her fierce features softened. “Let’s hope it doesn’t come to that.” And with a sudden, fluid motion, she dissolved into the night air, vanishing as though she had never been there at all.

Dion stood alone on the silent rooftop, the echo of her presence lingering like a faint vibration in his senses. The harbor’s lanterns shimmered far below, their reflections swaying on the dark water. "Interesting trick," he thought, a thin smile touching his lips. He would see her only once more and by then, the shadows over the city would be far deeper and far deadlier than either of them could have imagined.

Part 6: Urban Chaos

Decades passed without a single demon incursion. Dion’s life settled into the comfortable rhythm of balancing the quiet dominance of his Underground Network with the endless dramas of the city’s legal stage.

Some nights, he still let the shadows carry him across Newport Maeve. A unit he had formed followed him. Blood Blessed enforcers whose official purpose was to hunt renegade vampires. The ones who had succumbed to the Thirst. It was rare, but it happened. Their other, hidden, agenda was to watch for what The Entity instructed him to watch. It was hard, Dion understood, to be aware of something they had never seen. The Protector was also nowhere to be seen. No devils ever emerged after he met her. Only the small, familiar ills of urban life: petty theft, drunken brawls, the occasional gang war. Those were for the City Guard to handle, not for an elite Blood Blessed unit.

But one day, an hour after sundown, he saw it. A devil. The first he had laid eyes on since that war.

The devil prowled forward, claws rasping over stone. Then, without warning, the growl died in its throat and its fire guttered out, leaving only a hulking silhouette that crept in the silence that followed.

Curious, Dion followed from above, keeping to the darkness of the rooftops. His gaze, sharp as broken glass, tracked the creature's every motion. He was assessing it,cataloguing an enemy he hadn't faced in centuries.

A moment later, its target revealed itself. A boy was lingering at the mouth of the alley. Dion recognized him. A street con artist who played at charity. Sometimes he begged for an “ill child,” sometimes for a “new shelter,” each time armed with a new story and a hand-drawn flyer. The amounts were small, the faces forgettable, and so the city tolerated him. Dion knew him as he knew most who made their living in the cracks.

The boy waved off his latest "generous donors" and stepped into the alley, eyes on the coins in his hand… walking straight toward the two red lights in the dark.

Dion’s muscles tightened. He dropped soundlessly to the street.

The devil pounced, slamming into the boy and toppling him to the cobbles. It straddled him in an instant, claws poised at his throat, ready to tear it open—

—and Dion struck like a thunderbolt, seizing the creature and ripping it bodily away from its prey.

No roar, no hiss. Its eyes stayed locked on his with an unsettling clarity, as if calculating how to keep this fight unseen.

Its pupils throbbed with a sickly red light before it lunged in a blur of claws. The devil was fast and strong, Dion noted, almost on par with a vampire. But he was not merely one of the Blood Blessed. He was the first, and the strongest.

His twin curved blades flashed and he stepped aside and slashed alongside the devil's ribs. The creature passed next to him, dragged by its own momentum, but one of its talons tore the left sleeve of Dion's coat. It stopped and turned, but instead of attacking mindlessly again, it tilted its head and looked at the deep gash on its side. Black ichor leaked down its grotesque thigh and pooled on the cobbles below.

Dion expected another lash. Instead he noticed, unsettled, how the creature assessed him back. They were smart! They hadn't been like this, during the demon wars. Dion saw how the change happen in its burning eyes.

The brute understood it didn't have a chance. In a burst of motion, it fled from the alley, right toward the Market District.

Dion turned to the boy. “You hurt?”

Stiffly raising from the ground and wide-eyed, the boy shook his head.

“Name.”

“A–Aland.”

“Aland, find shelter. Tonight. Tomorrow—nineteen-hundred hours. Ironclad Tower. Wait for me.”

And before the boy could answer, Dion was gone.

He followed the wreckage left in the devil's wake. Toppled market stalls lined the path, alongside NightShift officers injured and reeking of blood. Ahead, he heard the commotion that followed the creature. Right then, he sensed another Blood Blessed and looked up. On the roof of a two-storey building nearby was one of the enforcers from his special unit. A vampire keeping watch for those lost to the Thirst. Now was the time for that "unofficial" part he had trained them for.

The enforcer caught his eye, waiting for orders. Dion gave a single, sharp gesture: intercept.

The vampire vanished into the dark.

Dion moved again, faster. Ordinary eyes could not see what was about to happen. Neither devil nor vampire should ever be a part of the street’s waking life.

Civic Center. Ready Room. 10 minutes earlier.

The jaunty pulse of a swing band drifted through the crowded main hall of the Civic Center, setting a cheerful mood for the night's event. A wedding was underway. A union shaped by senseless convenience. The son of a wealthy industrialist had chosen a wife. Chosen was the right word, for she had not chosen him.

She had been offered, or rather traded, for the promise of security and wealth, a bargain struck to lift her poor family from want. Slender and beautiful, with pale blue eyes and soft pink lips, she looked almost girlish with golden curls beneath her veil. But inside, she seethed.

Every passing second that drew her closer to a lifetime with him pressed heavier on her chest. In the ready room, surrounded by her maids of honor, she smiled faintly, then asked for a single minute alone. To breathe, she said. To ready herself.

They agreed, promising to wait in the main hall.

The door clicked shut behind them, and the smile fell away. Alone, she let her face twist in anger, sadness, desperation. She had no love for the groom. He was a rude, spoiled brat and she could not picture herself shackled to him for life. Tears welled, slipping free to blur her carefully painted makeup, the dark streaks tracing down the fine slope of her nose and along the curve of her soft chin, marring what should have been a radiant face.

She rose and approached the door. Outside, the atrium stretched ahead, dominated by a great bronze fountain whose curving figures seemed to point toward the wedding hall. She paused, staring down the path she was meant to take.

Then she turned around.

Her heels clicked against the marble. Measured at first, then faster, sharper, until the sound became a frantic rhythm driving her toward the soaring exit doors. She had no plan, no destination. Only the need to escape the suffocating inevitability.

The lamplight beyond the Civic Center spilled across the steps, guiding her into the open. She sprinted past the first corner, her gown snapping behind her in the cool night air, until she plunged into the shadow of a narrow alley and the noise of the celebration faded behind.

She had escaped one fate—only to collide with another.

Something massive loomed from the gloom. It hit her with the force of a battering ram, sending her sprawling. The white silk of her gown blossomed into red as her head struck the cobbles, blond hair spilling wildly over her shoulders.

Her vision blurred, narrowing to a tunnel. Through it, she caught a fleeting image of a man with dark curls and eyes that burned an unnatural red behind his round, shaded spectacles. For an instant those eyes fixed on her. She could read worry inside them. But he turned quickly and hurled himself at the beast that had struck her.

Then the world went black.

Civic Center back alley. Present time.

Dion reached the alley just in time to see a body sprawled across the cobbles. A girl in a white bridal gown, now splashed with crimson, lay still. Her blond hair was loose and strewn over a neck bent at an unnatural angle.

The devil was locked in combat with the vampire enforcer. Both were moving unnaturally fast: a flurry of claws clashing against arcs traced by curved blades. Dion’s lips curled in frustration.

“How did this night unravel into such chaos…” he muttered.

The enforcer fought hard but was being driven back, his footing slipping on the blood-slick stones. Dion stepped forward, ready to join—

—when a sudden gust of wind hit him, rattling the walls.

From above, the Angel descended in a rush of black feathers and golden light in her eyes. Her greatsword shuddered once, then unraveled into a whip of golden fire that coiled tight around the devil’s torso. With a flick of her arm, her cloak flared wide, and in a single heartbeat, both she and the creature were gone.

But not before her eyes found him.

They flicked between Dion and the broken girl at his feet, burning with fury that wasn't aimed at him. No, the devil would bear the full weight of that wrath.

Dion dropped to the girl's side. Shallow breaths. She was still alive. He didn't dare move her, not yet, not with her neck bent like that. The enforcer tore the hardened leather from his arm guard, snapping it cleanly in two to form a pair of rigid braces. He pressed them into Dion's hands. Dion worked slowly, packing the braces around her neck exactly as it lay, careful not to shift her by so much as a hair, then lashing them in place with his belt.

"Basement of the Blood Clinic," he ordered. "Now. And don't move her neck!"

The other vampire gathered her up carefully, one arm locked rigid beneath her neck, holding it steady with unwavering precision, granted by the Blood Blessing. Once he had her secured against him, he vanished into the night.

Already, figures were gathering at the mouth of the alley, drawn by the noise. Dion rose, stepping toward them with his hands slightly raised in a show of control.

“She’ll be fine,” he called out. “Up and running in no time.”

Part 7: Slumber of agony

Blood Clinic. Two levels below ground. The Morgue.

"She must not remember me," Dion said. He didn't want her to. The sight of her awoke an old, dread feeling in his gut.

Ages had passed, yet he had never learned to steel himself against this: watching an innocent woman die. The image of his first wife, the only one he had ever truly loved, bled in him like an old wound that never fully closed. He could still see her last breath leaving her lips, stolen by that hellish fever, her hand growing cold in his grasp. He could forget, perhaps. But he chose not to. That memory, in some strange way, kept him tethered to what was left of his humanity.

And this woman lay unconscious here because of him, because his choice to save the boy had drawn her into the night’s chaos. But Dion would not accept the bargain of one life for another, not while any chance remained to tip the scales back.

He looked down at her. Breath shallow. Spine twisted, likely broken. Even in this modern age, there was no coming back from that.

“But she will remember you, George,” Dion said to the vampire enforcer who had carried her here.

“I rarely make such exceptions, but she will live.” His gaze locked with the other’s. George—dark curls, spectacles he didn’t need—had his own ways of clinging to sanity through the endless years.

“The Dark Council doesn’t need to know about this,” Dion murmured, rubbing the bridge of his nose. “Or at least… they won’t know this version of events.”

He may have set the rules, but their enforcement was entrusted to others he deemed worthy. Because even he needed to be kept in check. Because of what was about to take place here and now.

The Dark Council had turned a blind eye to a few similar incidents in the past, but he knew better than to push his luck. Opposition, however faint, had begun to take shape. There were figures eager to test his authority, even to carry word of his actions to the Confederacy's highest leaders. That was trouble he could not afford now.

He glanced at her again, avoiding George’s eyes this time.

“You with me, George?” His voice was barely audible.

“Yes, Mr. Dion.” No hesitation. He had witnessed it firsthand. We may drink blood, Dion reflected, but we are not mindless animals. Not anymore.

Dion raised a hand. “Then leave. Later, we’ll make a story. Something for her ears, and for everyone else’s.”

George turned and left, carefully closing the door behind.

For a moment, Dion stood motionless, watching her. She was beautiful even now, looking innocent, as if asleep. Her hair, though disheveled, traced over her soft features. Absently he fixed a blond curl that revealed a small, snub nose. Somewhere, relatives would be looking for her. When she woke, she would have questions. Too many questions.

It would be hard to spin all this. But exile had taught him many things. Skills were lost once the One True's doctrine became the only lawful faith. Others were forgotten only because they had first been forbidden. They demanded sacrifice, and caused excruciating pain.

Her breath faltered. Time was gone. He bent over her and sank his fangs into the curve of her neck. Gently, almost tenderly, he drew only a little blood, then released the Blessing. The Blood Blessing that would carry her across the threshold into his world.

Her breathing slowed further, nearly ceasing. For a long minute, she hovered there. Dion waited, bracing himself.

Then it began.

Her body convulsed, wracked with tremors as the virus took hold. Dion had seen it a thousand times, witnessing every phase, every shudder. That's why he chained her wrists and ankles to the stretcher before all of this.

Her eyes snapped open, now alive with a crimson sheen. Dion was almost surprised. She had recovered too quickly. He hadn't wanted her awake.

His hands shoot up and he pressed his thumbs against her temples. With her head cradled in his palms, her body softened on the stretcher. Her limbs slackened as she slipped into darkness under his will.

There was no turning back. Not now. The veil of her consciousness parted, and Dion stepped through.

Like an unfading echo, he drifted down the corridors of her mind, unraveling every thread of who she had been. Memories crumbled into dust. When she woke, she would be a blank slate.

But he did not stop there. He pushed deeper into the very fabric of her body. Only because she was now like him could he reshape her. The cost was brutal: years of his vitality, and pain so intense it arched his spine and set fire through his nerves.

Her beauty sharpened under his hands. Cheekbones high and clean. The delicate roundness of her face honed to a graceful, predatory edge. Her hair darkened, gold giving way to deep jet black, streak by streak, as though night itself was spilling over her.

If she had been awake, the agony would have been unbearable. At least Dion spared her that.

He worked until she was no longer the girl who had walked into her wedding that night. Now she was a creature of the dark, forged in pain and sustained by his sacrifice.

At last, he broke contact and stumbled back, dropping to one knee. Pale. Shaking. The cold armor of his composure was shattered. It would take months to recover… and oceans of blood.

He unshackled her and turned toward the door, each step a slow limp. George would see to the rest. For now, Dion was spent—emptied in body and soul.

Part 8: The Hire

Ironclad Tower. The next day. 19:00 hours.

A boy stood before the tall facade. Its hybrid glass, half-reflective and half-transparent, mirrored his slight frame and worn clothes. Aland had no better to wear. In truth, he owned only one other set, and those were hardly finer.

Home was a crumbling shell at the edge of the Living District, a place he returned to only to sleep. The rest of his days were spent finding ways, any ways, to survive.

When the pale stranger who had pulled him from the jaws of… whatever that thing had been, told him to wait here tonight, he had obeyed. The pale man had saved his life, and Aland was sure the bill was coming due tonight. He could run or hide, but that was pointless against someone who came and went like smoke, and fought monsters born out of nightmares. He also had nowhere better to be... so he waited.

The time crept past nineteen hundred. Then half an hour more. Then an hour.

It was into the second hour that a man in a black suit finally stepped out from the entrance. Broad-shouldered, stony expression. Security, the boy mused. Without question.

"Aland Toreti." His voice was flat and indifferent, stating a fact rather than asking a question. Aland straightened, surprised that his last name was known.

"Y-Yes, sir," the boy replied a second later, with a slow nod.

"This way." A gesture toward the door.

Inside, the man led him to a small, secluded office just off the main reception. Behind a plain wooden desk sat his savior. Pale and haggard, he drank from a metal flask whose contents remained hidden, though his lips bore a faint, unnatural red.

“Ah, Aland,” the man greeted, his voice carrying a faint tremor. “Forgive the… condition you find me in. Few more days rest, and I’ll be a star.”

Aland, unsure of the proper response, simply nodded.

“Where are my manners?” The man’s tone lightened with effort. “Please, sit.” He gestured to the lone chair opposite the desk. “My name is Miroslav. Now we are no longer strangers.” He glanced toward the suited man. “You can leave us, Rox. Thank you.”

Once the door clicked shut, Miroslav leaned forward slightly. “Listen, Aland, I know what you do. And I think I can make use of it. How about I hire you?”

The boy stiffened. Hire? Him? A street rat?

“I need someone with… presentation skills,” Miroslav continued, sipping from the flask. “You’re young, but I see potential. And I’m willing to invest in you.”

Aland’s tension melted into a faint, almost impish smile. He took the chance... took the role. “Invest in me? You mean I get paid? Let’s talk numbers.”

Miroslav’s brows rose in surprise. Then his crimson lips curled in amusement. The ragged street boy had slipped effortlessly from beggar to bargainer, leaning forward onto the desk with his hands clasped as if he owned the moment.

“Oh, you are good," He leaned back, turning the chair a touch as he crossed one leg over the other, settling comfortably. "Yes… let’s talk numbers.”

Part 9: An Accord in Moonlight

High above Newport Maeve, Dion leaned against the terrace railing of his luxury apartment atop the Ironclad Consortium Tower. Moonlight slipped between restless clouds, silvering the rooftops and gilding the sharp lines of the cathedral spire across the district.

He was still recovering from the ordeal of transforming the unknown girl, and he had not seen her since. George had handled the rest. He wove a story, citing some older, poorly documented case of a transformation gone wrong. He also chose a name: Shalina. Not bad. He had even seen to the bureaucratic rites that came with binding someone to the night.

Dion took a measured sip from the metal flask in his hand. Strength was returning, but slowly.

Then movement caught his attention. A shadow peeled away from the cathedral’s highest point. Wings spread wide. Not a statue. Her. And she was making certain he saw her.

She stepped from the stone into the void, and her wings clapped once, carrying her into a slow, sure glide. Dion watched the elegant rhythm of her flight, a dangerous beauty in every measured beat, until she hovered before the terrace and stopped. She just stayed there, suspended, the soft downdraft of her wings brushing against him.

They held one another’s gaze for a long moment. Then Dion tilted his head toward the small brass table where a whiskey bottle and single glass waited.

“A drink?” he asked, with a faint, knowing smile.

Her laughter was low and melodic, lingering in the air. “Very funny, Dion…”

Another pause. “...Is she dead?”

The smile dimmed. “No.. Barely. She’ll live.”

“Who is she?”

He shrugged. “Does it matter? The important part is that she made it.”

He offered nothing further. The details surrounding this girl would remain buried; the Protector would never approve what he had done. Or would she? Dion wasn’t willing to take that chance.

“I suppose.” She hesitated, eyes flicking briefly to the horizon before returning to his. “Listen… I owe you an apology. That night, there were too many incursions. I couldn’t be everywhere at once… but you helped.” Her wings edged her closer until she was nearly within reach. “As you were meant to.”

She extended her hand. A handshake. Dion thought of their exchange on the rooftop above the harbor, then the look in her eyes when she swept the devil away in the back alley. She offered a truce and more importantly—acknowledgment.

Dion took it, his grip steady, eyes lingering on hers a moment too long. Her skin was unexpectedly smooth and warm for a being who wielded a blade so fiercely. For the briefest heartbeat, a sensation like the faintest crackle of electricity brushed his fingertips. Subtle, gentle, almost enjoyable, as though her touch carried a quiet current sparking against him before slipping away. Real… or imagined, he couldn’t tell. Yet something in her expression softened. Barely, but enough for him to wonder if she had felt it too. Whatever it was, he buried it quickly, locking the moment away where it could not distract him.

“Something’s wrong in this city,” she said after a moment, her voice quieter now. “These creatures are appearing more often, and I can’t find the source… as if something is veiling them from me.”

“My spies whisper about a new cult,” Dion replied. “No name or origin. They erase rivals and absorb their followers. The structure is strange. Cells that think they’re in command, but are just pieces of something larger. Fully decentralized.”

She turned toward the Moon Sea, where the silver reflection of the moon rippled in the water. “I’m forbidden to act directly. I can only clean the aftermath.”

“I’ll keep watch,” Dion said.

She looked back over her shoulder at him, her expression softer now. “We may not see each other again. Even now, I’m not supposed to be here.”

He gave a small shrug. “Rules are rules.”

That earned him a genuine smile. “Then I’ll miss breaking them with you.”

With a single beat of her wings, she rose from the terrace. “Farewell, Dion.”

“Farewell,” he said, lifting the flask in salute.

This time, she didn’t vanish into her cloak of invisibility. She let him watch her go, the sheen of her slick black feathers catching stray threads of moonlight, each beat of her wings a fluid, deliberate motion. The air seemed to hum faintly with their rhythm, a dance of shadow and silver that held his gaze long after she had pulled away from the terrace. It felt almost as though she meant for him to see it, as though she knew he enjoyed the sight.

Only when she reached the cathedral spire did she merge again with its shadowed stonework.

A slow, unexpected shiver traced Dion’s skin—an echo of a feeling he thought had been lost to him forever.

He felt… alone. And, strangely, he minded.
